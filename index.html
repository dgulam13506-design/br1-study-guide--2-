Chapter 2 · Week 1

The Scientific Approach

Hallmarks of science, the hypothetico-deductive method & research philosophies
📌 Core Definition

Scientific research is an organised, systematic, data-based, critical, objective inquiry into a specific problem, conducted in a step-by-step logical manner. It is not based on hunches or intuition alone.

⭐ The 8 Hallmarks of Scientific Research
1. Purposiveness
Research starts with a clear, definite purpose or aim. The researcher has a focused direction from the outset.
2. Rigor
A good theoretical base and sound methodological design are critical. Carefulness, scrupulousness, and exactitude in all procedures.
3. Testability
Hypotheses developed from the research must be testable using available statistical methods and empirical data.
4. Replicability
Results should be reproducible when the same methods are applied in similar conditions by other researchers.
5. Precision & Confidence
Precision = closeness to the true value (standard error). Confidence = probability the true value falls within the estimate range (e.g. 95%).
6. Objectivity
Conclusions are based on empirical facts from data, not on the researcher's personal values, emotions, or biases.
7. Generalizability
The scope to which findings from one study can be applied to other settings, contexts, and populations.
8. Parsimony
Economy in explanation — the simplest model that adequately explains the phenomenon is preferred over complex ones.
🔄 The Hypothetico-Deductive Method (7 Steps)
Key Idea

Starts with a theory, generates testable hypotheses, collects data, and uses results to confirm or disconfirm the theory. The most widely used scientific method in business research.

1
Identify a broad problem area — Observe something that warrants investigation in the workplace.
2
Define the problem statement — Narrow it into a clear, focused, researchable question.
3
Develop hypotheses — Formulate testable, tentative statements about expected relationships between variables.
4
Determine measures — Decide how to operationalise and measure the variables in each hypothesis.
5
Data collection — Gather data from appropriate sources using the chosen instruments.
6
Data analysis — Analyse the collected data using appropriate statistical techniques.
7
Interpretation of data — Interpret results, accept or reject hypotheses, draw conclusions about the problem.
🔭 Alternative Research Philosophies

Positivism

Assumes an external, objective reality independent of the observer. Knowledge comes from observable facts. Quantitative, value-free methods. Researcher is independent from what is studied.

Constructionism

Reality is socially constructed through interaction. Focuses on how people make sense of the world. Qualitative, interpretive methods. Studies the accounts people give of their experiences.

Critical Realism

Real structures in the world exist independently of us, but our knowledge is always provisional. Distinguishes between observable events and the underlying mechanisms that cause them.

Pragmatism

Not committed to one philosophy. Chooses methods based on what works best for the question. Supports mixed methods. Research is evaluated by its practical consequences.

Chapter 6 · Week 2

Elements of Research Design

Blueprint for data collection, measurement, and analysis
📌 Definition

A research design is a blueprint or plan for the collection, measurement, and analysis of data, created to answer the research questions. It ensures data collected are relevant and analysable.

🗺️ Research Strategies
Experiments
Researcher manipulates independent variables and controls the environment to establish cause-and-effect relationships.
Survey Research
Data collected from a sample via questionnaires or interviews to describe, compare, or explain attitudes and behaviours.
Ethnography
Researcher immerses themselves in a natural environment over extended time. Uses participant observation and field notes.
Case Studies
In-depth investigation of one or a few real-life cases. Useful for understanding complex phenomena in context.
Grounded Theory
Theory built inductively from data collected in the field. The theory is literally "grounded" in the empirical data.
Action Research
Conducted collaboratively with practitioners to solve a practical problem while simultaneously generating knowledge.
⚙️ Key Design Dimensions

Researcher Interference

  • Minimal (correlational) — no manipulation, studies things as they are
  • Moderate (ex post facto) — examines cause after the fact
  • Maximum (experiment) — manipulates the independent variable

Study Setting

  • Contrived (lab) — artificial; high internal validity, low external validity
  • Non-contrived (field) — natural setting; high external validity, harder to control variables

Unit of Analysis

  • Individual — each person's data analysed separately
  • Dyad — two-person pairs (e.g. supervisor–subordinate)
  • Group — teams, departments
  • Organisation — whole companies
  • Culture — societies, nations

Time Horizon

  • Cross-sectional — one point in time; faster, cheaper; snapshot
  • Longitudinal — multiple time points; tracks change; establishes temporal ordering

Chapter 9 · Week 3

Administering Questionnaires

Design, types, wording principles & data collection ethics
📌 Definition

A questionnaire is a preformulated written set of questions to which respondents record their answers, usually within closely defined alternatives. Used to collect large amounts of quantitative data.

📋 Types of Questionnaires

Personally Administered

Distributed and collected in person. Researcher can clarify doubts. High response rate. Can ensure confidentiality on the spot. Good for sensitive topics.

Mail Questionnaires

Sent by post. Wide geographic reach, low cost. Very low response rates. No one to clarify ambiguities. Slow turnaround.

Electronic / Online

Sent via email or web platform. Fast, cheap, can reach large samples. Risk of sampling bias (internet users only). Can use branching logic.

⚠️ Common Questionnaire Pitfalls (Exam Favourites!)
Double-barreled
Asks two questions at once: "How satisfied are you with your salary AND benefits?" — must be split into two separate questions.
Leading question
Wording that suggests the expected answer: "Don't you agree this policy is fair?" — introduces systematic bias.
Loaded question
Uses emotionally charged words that trigger a biased reaction, e.g. asking about a "radical" or "controversial" policy.
Ambiguous wording
Vague terms like "often" or "many" are interpreted differently by different respondents — use specific frequencies.
Recall dependence
Asking respondents to recall events from the distant past leads to memory errors and inaccurate data.
Social desirability bias
Respondents answer to appear socially acceptable rather than giving their true response.
📐 Question Types
TypeDescriptionExample
Open-endedRespondent answers freely in own words"Describe your experience with remote work."
DichotomousOnly two optionsYes / No
Multiple choiceFixed list of options, choose one or moreA, B, C, D
Rating scaleDegree of agreement/intensity1 = Strongly Disagree … 5 = Strongly Agree
RankingOrder items by preference or importanceRank 1–5 from most to least important
Sequencing Rule

Start with general, non-sensitive questions → move to specific → place sensitive/demographic questions (age, income, religion) at the end.

Chapter 11 · Week 4

Measurement: Operational Definition

How to translate abstract concepts into measurable variables
📌 Definition

Measurement = the assignment of numbers or other symbols to characteristics (attributes) of objects according to a prespecified set of rules.

🔬 Operationalisation
Operational Definition
Specifies exactly HOW an abstract concept will be measured in a study — reducing it to observable, measurable indicators. Example: "motivation" → score on a validated 12-item motivation scale.
Conceptual Definition
A theoretical, dictionary-type definition of what a construct IS. Describes the meaning but not how to measure it.
Dimensions
The sub-components or facets of a concept. "Achievement motivation" has dimensions of work orientation, mastery, and competitiveness.
Elements
The specific questionnaire items or indicators used to measure each dimension. Elements make up the scale items.
🔄 The Operationalisation Process
1
Identify the concept to be measured (e.g. "employee loyalty")
2
Identify the dimensions of the concept (e.g. attitudinal loyalty, behavioural loyalty)
3
Define elements for each dimension (specific questions that capture each dimension)
4
Create the measurement scale (e.g. 5-point Likert scale items for each element)
⚠️ What Operationalisation Is NOT

Operationalisation is not just a conceptual definition. It must specify how you will actually measure the concept. Without operationalisation, abstract constructs cannot be tested empirically.

Chapter 12 · Week 4

Scaling, Reliability & Validity

Types of scales and assessing measurement quality
📏 The Four Scale Types (NOIR)
ScalePropertiesExampleStatistics allowed
NominalCategories only; no order, no distanceGender, nationality, job typeMode, frequencies, chi-square
OrdinalRanked order; distances not equalRankings, competitive positionsMedian, percentile, rank correlation
IntervalEqual distances; no true zeroTemperature (°C), IQ, year of birthMean, SD, correlation, regression
RatioEqual distances + true zeroAge, salary, sales volume, weightAll statistics including ratios
⚖️ Rating Scales
Likert Scale
Respondents indicate degree of agreement with statements (1 = Strongly Disagree to 5 = Strongly Agree). Items are summed into a composite score. Most common in business research.
Semantic Differential
Bipolar adjectives at opposite ends of a continuum (e.g. Good ←——→ Bad). Used to measure attitudes, brand image, or perceptions.
Numerical Scale
Like semantic differential but uses numbers rather than words at the anchors. Respondents select a number along the continuum.
Itemised Rating Scale
Provides specific numbered categories each with a brief verbal description; respondents select the category that best fits.
🔍 Reliability & Validity

Reliability

A measure is reliable if it consistently produces the same results.

  • Stability (test-retest): Same results when administered twice to same group
  • Internal consistency: Items measuring same construct correlate (Cronbach's alpha ≥ 0.70)

Validity

A measure is valid if it actually measures what it claims to measure.

  • Content: Items cover all facets of the concept
  • Criterion-related: Correlates with an external criterion
  • Construct: Measures the theoretical construct correctly
⚠️ Key Relationship

A measure can be reliable without being valid (consistently measures the wrong thing). But a valid measure must also be reliable. Validity is the higher-order criterion.

🔄 Reflective vs Formative Scales

Reflective Scale

The latent construct causes the items. If the construct changes, all items should change in the same direction. Items must correlate with each other. Example: depression items are all caused by the underlying depression construct.

Formative Scale

The items together define the construct — items "form" the construct. Items don't need to correlate. Example: SES = income + education + job status. Changing one affects SES even if the others stay constant.

Chapter 13 · Week 5

Sampling

Selecting the right participants to represent the population
📚 Key Terminology
Population
The entire group of people, events, or things about which the researcher wants to draw conclusions.
Element
A single member of the population (one person, one company, one transaction).
Sample
A subset of the population. Research is conducted on the sample; results are generalised to the population.
Sampling Unit
The unit chosen for inclusion in the sample — could be a person, household, or organisation.
Subject
A single member of the sample (the actual participant in the study).
Sampling Frame
The actual list of sampling units from which the sample is drawn (e.g. employee database, phone directory).
🎲 Probability Sampling
Definition

Every member of the population has a known, non-zero probability of being selected. Allows for statistical generalisation to the population.

Simple Random Sampling (SRS)
Every element has an equal and independent chance. Uses random number generators. Most basic probability method.
Systematic Sampling
Every k-th element from a list (e.g. every 10th person). Requires a sampling frame. Risk: periodicity bias.
Stratified Random Sampling
Population divided into strata by a characteristic (e.g. gender). Random sample from each stratum. More representative.
Cluster Sampling
Population divided into natural clusters (e.g. cities). Clusters randomly selected; elements within studied. Cheaper for large geographic populations.
Double Sampling
A subsample is drawn from an initial sample for more detailed study. Used when extra data collection is expensive.
👤 Non-Probability Sampling
Definition

Not every member has a known selection probability. Cannot statistically generalise to the population. Used when probability sampling is not feasible or sufficient for the purpose.

Convenience Sampling
Select whoever is most accessible. Cheapest and fastest. Lowest external validity. Used in exploratory research.
Purposive / Judgmental
Researcher deliberately selects elements believed to be representative or useful. Requires domain expertise.
Quota Sampling
Like stratified sampling but selection within strata is non-random. Ensures subgroup representation without true randomness.
Snowball Sampling
Participants refer further participants. Ideal for hard-to-reach populations (e.g. undocumented workers, rare conditions).
📊 Precision & Confidence

Precision

How close the sample estimate is to the true population value. Measured by the standard error (margin of error). More precision = smaller margin = larger sample needed.

Confidence

The probability that the true population value falls within the stated range. Common levels: 90%, 95%, 99%. Higher confidence = larger sample needed.

⚠️ Trade-off

For a fixed sample size, you cannot simultaneously maximise both precision and confidence — increasing one reduces the other. Larger samples improve both.

Welcome to Economics

Scarcity, choice, the PPF, and the scope of economics
📌 Core Idea

Economics is the study of how humans make choices under scarcity. Because resources are limited but wants are unlimited, every choice involves a trade-off.

📚 Key Concepts
Scarcity
Resources (time, money, land, labour) are limited relative to unlimited human wants. Scarcity forces choices.
Microeconomics
Studies individual decision-makers: consumers, firms, and specific markets. Focuses on prices and quantities in particular markets.
Macroeconomics
Studies the whole economy: GDP, inflation, unemployment, economic growth, and government policy.
Factors of Production
Land, Labour, Capital, and Entrepreneurship — the four inputs used to produce goods and services.
PPF (Production Possibilities Frontier)
A graph showing all maximum combinations of two goods an economy can produce when resources are fully and efficiently used. Points inside = inefficient; points outside = unattainable; on the curve = efficient.

Choice in a World of Scarcity

Opportunity cost, budget constraints & comparative advantage (pp. 27–33)
📌 Core Idea

Every choice has an opportunity cost — the value of the next best alternative foregone. There is no such thing as a free lunch.

📚 Key Concepts
Opportunity Cost
The value of the best alternative you give up when making a choice. It is the true economic cost of any decision.
Budget Constraint
A line showing all combinations of two goods a consumer can afford given their income and the prices of the goods. Income = P₁×Q₁ + P₂×Q₂.
Comparative Advantage
The ability to produce a good at a lower opportunity cost than others. Basis for specialisation and mutually beneficial trade.
Diminishing Marginal Utility
Each additional unit of a good consumed provides less extra satisfaction than the previous unit.

Chapter 3 · Week 3

Demand and Supply

Laws of demand and supply, equilibrium, shifts in curves (pp. 47–80)
📌 Key Rule

A change in a good's own price → movement along the curve. A change in anything else → shift of the entire curve.

📚 Key Concepts
Law of Demand
Price ↑ → Quantity Demanded ↓ (inverse relationship), ceteris paribus.
Law of Supply
Price ↑ → Quantity Supplied ↑ (positive relationship), ceteris paribus.
Equilibrium
Where Qd = Qs. The market clears — no surplus or shortage. Price has no tendency to change.
Surplus (Excess Supply)
Price is above equilibrium: Qs > Qd. Price falls back toward equilibrium.
Shortage (Excess Demand)
Price is below equilibrium: Qd > Qs. Price rises back toward equilibrium.
Substitutes & Complements
Substitutes: replace each other (butter/margarine). Complements: used together (cars/petrol). Price of one affects demand for the other.
⚠️ Demand Curve Shifters

Elasticity

PED, PES, income elasticity — up to p.122
📌 Formula

PED = % change in Quantity Demanded ÷ % change in Price. Always negative (law of demand) but usually expressed as absolute value.

📐 Elasticity Values

PED = 1Unit elastic% changes are equal—
PED > 1ElasticQuantity very responsiveLuxury holidays, designer goods

📚 Key Concepts
Factors making demand MORE elastic
Many substitutes · Luxury item · Large share of income · More time to adjust · Narrowly defined market
Factors making demand MORE inelastic
Few substitutes · Necessity · Small share of income · Short time to adjust · Broadly defined market
PED and Total Revenue
Elastic demand: price ↑ → TR falls. Inelastic demand: price ↑ → TR rises. Unit elastic: price change has no effect on TR.
Income Elasticity of Demand
YED = % ΔQd ÷ % ΔIncome. Positive = normal good (demand rises with income). Negative = inferior good.

Chapter 7 · Week 6

Production and Cost

Fixed & variable costs, MC, ATC, economies of scale — up to p.173
📌 Golden Rule

Profit is maximised where Marginal Revenue = Marginal Cost. This applies to every market structure.

📚 Cost Concepts
Fixed Cost (FC)
Does not change with output. Paid even if nothing is produced. E.g. rent, insurance, machinery.
Variable Cost (VC)
Changes with output. Zero when nothing is produced. E.g. raw materials, hourly wages.
Marginal Cost (MC)
Extra cost of producing one more unit. MC = ΔTC ÷ ΔQ. MC curve is U-shaped due to diminishing returns.
Average Total Cost (ATC)
ATC = TC ÷ Q = AFC + AVC. The MC curve crosses ATC at its minimum point.
Economies of Scale
Long-run ATC falls as output increases. Larger scale → lower cost per unit (e.g. bulk buying, specialisation).
Diseconomies of Scale
Long-run ATC rises as output increases beyond optimal size. Coordination problems, management inefficiency.

Chapter 8 · Week 7

Perfect Competition

Price takers, profit maximisation, shutdown point — up to p.199
📚 Characteristics

Features of Perfect Competition

  • Many buyers and sellers
  • Identical (homogeneous) products
  • Free entry and exit
  • Perfect information
  • Firms are price takers (P = MR)

Long-Run Equilibrium

  • P = MR = MC = ATC
  • Economic profit = 0
  • Firms earn normal profit only
  • No incentive to enter or exit
  • Productive and allocative efficiency
⚠️ Shutdown Rule

Short run: shut down when P < AVC (can't cover variable costs — better to produce nothing). Long run: exit when P < ATC (making economic losses).

Long-Run Equilibrium (PC)
P = MR = MC = ATC and π = 0

Free entry and exit drives economic profit to zero. Both productive efficiency (min ATC) and allocative efficiency (P = MC) hold.

Chapter 9 · Week 7

Monopoly

Price maker, barriers to entry, deadweight loss — up to p.223
📌 Key Difference

In perfect competition: P = MC. In monopoly: P > MC (allocative inefficiency). The monopolist restricts output to raise price above marginal cost.

📚 Key Concepts
Monopoly
Single seller, no close substitutes, price maker. Faces downward-sloping demand curve for the whole market.
Barriers to Entry
Patents, licences, control of key resources, high fixed costs (natural monopoly), network effects.
MR < Price
To sell one more unit, the monopolist must cut price on ALL units sold. So MR < P for every unit.
Deadweight Loss
The efficiency lost because the monopolist produces less than the socially optimal quantity and charges P > MC.
Natural Monopoly
One firm can serve the whole market at lower cost than multiple firms — due to very high fixed costs and economies of scale.
Price Discrimination
Charging different prices to different consumers based on willingness to pay (e.g. student discounts, peak pricing).

Ch.10–11 · Week 7

Monopolistic Competition & Oligopoly

Differentiation, game theory, concentration ratios — up to p.266

Monopolistic Competition

  • Many firms, differentiated products
  • Free entry and exit
  • Some pricing power (downward-sloping demand)
  • Long run: zero economic profit
  • Excess capacity — P > MC (inefficient)

Oligopoly

  • Few large firms dominate
  • High barriers to entry
  • Strategic interdependence
  • Collusion possible (but often illegal)
  • Game theory applies
🎮 Prisoner's Dilemma

Each player has a dominant strategy to defect (confess/undercut). Acting in self-interest leads to a collectively worse outcome than if they had cooperated — the core logic of why cartels break down.

📊 Concentration Ratios
Four-Firm Concentration Ratio
Combined market share of the 4 largest firms. Below 40% = competitive; 40–60% = oligopolistic; above 60% = highly concentrated.
Collusion
Secret agreement to fix prices, limit output, or divide markets. Creates cartel behaviour. Usually illegal under competition law.

Ch.12–13 · Week 4

Externalities & Public Goods

Market failures, Pigouvian taxes, subsidies & public goods

Negative Externality (Ch.12)

  • Cost imposed on third parties
  • Market overproduces (private cost < social cost)
  • Fix: Pigouvian tax, regulations, permits
  • Example: factory pollution

Positive Externality (Ch.13)

  • Benefit enjoyed by third parties
  • Market underproduces (private benefit < social benefit)
  • Fix: subsidy, government provision
  • Example: vaccinations, education
📌 Social Optimum

The efficient output is where Marginal Social Cost = Marginal Social Benefit. Free markets miss this when externalities exist.

📚 Public Goods
Non-excludable
Cannot prevent anyone from using the good, even if they don't pay. E.g. national defence, street lighting.
Non-rival
One person's use does not reduce availability for others. E.g. a TV broadcast — one more viewer doesn't affect others.
Free-rider Problem
People benefit without paying. Private firms can't profit from public goods → market underprovides → government must supply.

Chapter 1: Excel basics

The golden rule: never type numbers in formulas

Every number (VAT rate, bonus %, monthly payment) must live in its own cell. Your formula references that cell. That way, changing one cell updates everything automatically.

Wrong:

=D1*0.21

Correct:

=D1*D2

Where D2 contains 0.21. If the VAT changes, you only update D2 — all formulas recalculate automatically.

Cell references: relative vs absolute

When you copy a formula, Excel shifts all references automatically. This is called a relative reference.

=A2*B2 → copied to row 3 becomes =A3*B3

If you need a reference to stay fixed, add a $ sign. Press F4 to cycle through all four options:

SyntaxWhat's lockedWhen to use it
A1NothingStandard row-by-row calculations
$A1Column onlyColumn stays fixed, rows vary
A$1Row onlyRow stays fixed, columns vary
$A$1BothOne fixed value used everywhere (e.g. VAT rate)
⚠️ Only use $ when the formula will be copied AND the reference must not shift. Adding unnecessary $ loses points on the exam.

Referencing another sheet

='SheetName'!CellAddress

Example: pull cell B1 from the Input sheet:

='Input'!B1

Or combine with locking:

='Rates'!$C$9
💡 Easiest method: start your formula, then click the other sheet tab and click the cell. Excel writes the reference for you.

Chapter 2: Advanced Excel

Data validation — controlling what gets entered

Two main types used in your exam:

Dropdown list

Data → Data Validation → List → set source to a cell range (e.g. =CustomerData!$A$2:$A$26). You can also add an input message and an error alert.

Restricted number input

Data → Data Validation → Whole Number → Between → set min/max. Add an input message ("Enter a value between 1 and 10") and error alert.

Conditional formatting

Home → Conditional Formatting → Highlight Cell Rules. You can colour cells based on value, text match, date, etc. Useful for making patterns visible at a glance — e.g. red for overdue invoices, green for paid.

For the exam: make sure your formatting rules reference cell addresses (not hardcoded numbers) so they update if the threshold changes.

IF function

=IF(condition; value_if_true; value_if_false)

Example — pass/fail based on a grade:

=IF(E2>=C$12; B$13; B$14)

You can nest IF inside IF for multiple conditions, or combine with AND/OR:

=IF(AND(B3>=4; B3<=16); "school age"; "not school age")
=IF(OR(C3="blue"; C3="white"); $H$3; $H$4)

VLOOKUP

=VLOOKUP(lookup_value; table_array; col_index; range_lookup)
ArgumentMeaning
lookup_valueWhat you're searching for
table_arrayThe table to search in (lock with $ when copying)
col_indexWhich column to return (1 = first column of table)
range_lookupFALSE = exact match | TRUE = approximate (for ranges)
Use FALSE for names, codes, text. Use TRUE only for numeric ranges where the value falls between two boundaries (e.g. date ranges, age bands, star table).

HLOOKUP

=HLOOKUP(lookup_value; table_array; row_index; range_lookup)

Same as VLOOKUP but searches across a row instead of down a column. Use it when your table is horizontal (headers in a row, data below).

Example — look up account type rate from a horizontal rates table:

=HLOOKUP(E3; Rates!B11:C12; 2; 0)

Nested VLOOKUP (the trickiest one)

Use one VLOOKUP inside another when you need a two-step lookup — first get an intermediate value, then use it to find the final answer.

=VLOOKUP(VLOOKUP(B3; CustomerData!A:E; 3; FALSE); Rates!B2:E7; 4; TRUE)

Inner lookup gets the opening date. Outer lookup uses that date to find the star colour.

XLOOKUP (newer, more flexible)

=XLOOKUP(lookup_value; lookup_array; return_array; "not found")

Advantages over VLOOKUP: can search left/right, returns multiple columns, defaults to exact match, has a built-in "not found" message, doesn't break if you insert columns.

=XLOOKUP(F1; C3:C7; A3:B7; "no department in this city")
⚠️ XLOOKUP only works in newer Excel versions. Stick to VLOOKUP/HLOOKUP unless explicitly asked for XLOOKUP.

Chapter 3: MS Access

What is a database?

An organised collection of data structured into tables. Unlike Excel (one sheet of data), a database links multiple related tables together.

TermMeaningExample
FieldA column — one piece of infoCustomerName, Balance
RecordA row — one complete entryOne customer's full details
TableAll records for one entityCustomers, Orders, Products
Primary keyA unique identifier per recordStudentNumber, CustomerID

Primary key

Every table needs a primary key — a field (or combination of fields) that uniquely identifies each record. No two records can have the same primary key value.

Example: your student number at AMSIB is a primary key. No other student has the same number, so it uniquely identifies you in the database.

Relationships between tables

Tables are linked via a shared field. The primary key of one table appears as a foreign key in another. This avoids storing the same data in multiple places.

Two integrity rules to know:

Rule 1 A primary key field cannot be empty (null).

Rule 2 — Referential integrity A foreign key must either match an existing primary key in the related table, or be empty. You cannot add a record that refers to a non-existent parent record.

Queries — retrieving data

A query retrieves specific records based on criteria you set. Key query types:

TypeWhat it doesExample criteria
ProjectionShow only certain fieldsSelect only Name and City
SelectionFilter by conditionBalance > 1000
WildcardPartial text matchLike "A*" (starts with A)
Date criteriaFilter by dateBetween #1/1/2020# And #12/31/2020#
ANDBoth conditions must be trueCity = "Amsterdam" AND Balance > 500
OREither condition can be trueCity = "Amsterdam" OR City = "Rotterdam"
ParameterAsks for input when run[Enter customer name:]
In the criteria grid: AND conditions go on the same row. OR conditions go on separate rows.

Forms

A form is a user-friendly interface for entering data into a table. Instead of working directly in the raw table, a form displays one record at a time in a clean layout. Create via: Create → Form.

Reports

A report formats data for printing or presenting. Create via: Create → Report Wizard. You can group records, add totals/averages, and choose the layout. Reports are read-only — you cannot edit data in a report.

SQL basics

Access queries work in SQL behind the scenes. You can switch to SQL view to see or write the query directly.

SELECT CustomerName, Balance FROM Customers WHERE Balance > 1000 ORDER BY Balance DESC;

Key words: SELECT (what fields), FROM (which table), WHERE (condition), ORDER BY (sort), GROUP BY (for aggregation).
